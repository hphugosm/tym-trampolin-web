Artificial intelligence has become an everyday part of studying, allowing students to solve difficult problems, understand complicated topics and complete assignments faster than ever before. Whenever we get stuck, the answer is usually just a few clicks away. However, while AI makes studying easier, recent research suggests that this convenience might come at an unexpected cost. What if getting help too quickly is actually preventing us from learning?

In research published in 2026, Stefanos Poulidis, Hamsa Bastani and Osbert Bastani investigated how access to artificial intelligence influences learning. Over a period of 12 weeks, they studied more than 200 chess-club students who were learning with AI assistance. Both groups received guidance from AI, but one group had the additional option of requesting help whenever they wanted. Surprisingly, students who could ask for extra assistance improved their chess performance by approximately **30%, while those with more restricted access improved by 64%.**

These results highlight an important aspect of learning known as *productive struggle*. When we encounter a difficult problem and attempt to solve it independently, our brains must actively work with information, consider different possibilities and recognize mistakes. Although this process can be frustrating, it plays an important role in developing our understanding. By immediately providing answers, AI can sometimes remove precisely the mental effort that makes learning effective.

Fortunately, this doesn't mean we should stop using ChatGPT or other AI tools. Instead, we could change the way we interact with them. One simple idea is the **30-second rule**: whenever you encounter a difficult question, spend at least 30 seconds trying to solve it yourself before asking AI for assistance. Even after that, consider requesting a small hint rather than a complete solution, allowing yourself to continue working through the problem independently.

<!-- shake:challenge -->

While the researchers did not specifically test a 30-second waiting period, this simple experiment applies the central lesson of their findings: the timing and amount of assistance can matter just as much as the assistance itself.

AI has enormous potential to improve education, but its greatest value may not lie in how quickly it provides answers. Sometimes, the most effective learning assistant is one that encourages us to think independently, even when doing so takes a little longer.

<!-- shake:closing -->

Perhaps the smartest AI tutor isn't the one that always knows the answer, but the one that knows when not to give it to you.
